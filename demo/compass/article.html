How to delegate without disappearing

Handing work off is easy. Handing it off so it comes back better, without you hovering or vanishing, is a skill. Here’s the one I teach every new manager.

Nadia WhitcombeLeadership coach September 29, 2026 9 min read

Most new managers delegate in one of two ways. They hand over a task with so much instruction that they might as well have done it themselves, or they hand it over with almost none and then quietly rescue it at 10pm the night before it’s due. Both feel like delegation. Neither builds a team.

The fix isn’t to delegate more. It’s to be explicit about how much of the decision you’re handing over, and to agree in advance how you’ll stay in touch.

The delegation ladder

Every piece of work you hand off sits somewhere on a ladder of five levels. Most friction comes from the two of you standing on different rungs without knowing it.

  1. 1Do exactly this. You’ve decided; they execute. Right for urgent, high-risk or very new work.
  2. 2Research and report back. They gather options; you decide.
  3. 3Recommend, then I’ll decide. They own the thinking; you keep the final call.
  4. 4Decide and tell me. They decide; you hear about it before it goes out.
  5. 5Own it. They decide and act. You hear about it in your next one-to-one, if at all.

Say the level out loud when you hand something over: “This one’s a three: bring me a recommendation by Thursday.” It takes four seconds and removes most of the guesswork.

Agree the check-ins up front

Hovering happens when you don’t know how it’s going. Disappearing happens when you’re too busy to ask. Both are solved by agreeing, at the moment of handover, when you’ll next look at it together. For most work that’s three points: a quick shape check early on, a midpoint review, and a final look before it ships.

The early check is the one people skip and the one that matters most. Ten minutes on day two catches the misunderstanding that would otherwise cost a week.

Climb one rung at a time

Move each person up the ladder deliberately. When a level three goes well twice, try a level four on the next similar task, and tell them that’s what you’re doing. People grow fastest when they can see the next rung.

And when something goes wrong, step down one rung for that kind of work, not all the way to the bottom. A mistake is information about the level, not about the person.

Try this week

  • Pick one task you’re still doing that someone else could own.
  • Hand it over with a level from the ladder, said out loud.
  • Put the three check-ins in both your calendars before you leave the room.
