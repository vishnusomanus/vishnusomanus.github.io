October 20, 2026 · Research

Fifty homes, one winter: what the data says

We logged every half hour in fifty pilot homes last winter. On average 84% of their heat was bought at the cheap rate, and no home ran out of hot water.

ReadClose

Our pilot homes range from a one-bedroom apartment in downtown Bangor to a drafty 1890s farmhouse in Holden. Between November and March each logged its store temperature, heat delivered and electricity bought, every half hour.

Across all fifty, 84% of the electricity used for heating and hot water was bought in the cheap hours. The best home managed 96%; the worst, a large house with an undersized prototype, managed 61%. That house now has a bigger store.

These are averages from a small pilot, not a promise for your home. We will publish the full data set, anonymized, with our next research note.

Heatloop 40 joins the range for larger homes

A taller, wider store with 40 kWh of heat for large houses and homes with more than one bathroom.

ReadClose

Large homes in eastern Maine can use more than 150 kWh of heat on a cold day, which is more than a Heatloop 28 can shift on its own. The Heatloop 40 adds a wider store, a 9 kW element and faster hot water.

July 14, 2026 · Research

How we test a store for 10,000 cycles

Ten thousand melts and freezes is about twenty-seven years of daily use. Here is how we compress that into a few months.

ReadClose

A Heatloop melts and freezes its cells about once a day. To be confident a store will last, we cycle sample cells much faster in a rig that heats and cools them with oil, logging capacity every hundred cycles.

Our current cell design has passed 10,000 cycles with less than 4% loss of capacity in testing. We also open up returned cells from pilot homes to check that the field matches the lab.

April 21, 2026 · Product

A smarter charging schedule, delivered over the air

Pilot homes now plan tomorrow’s charging using the weather forecast as well as prices. No visit needed.

ReadClose

Until now, each Heatloop charged fully in the cheapest hours every night. The new schedule reads the next day’s forecast and how the home used heat last week, so mild days need less charging and cold snaps get a top-up after lunch.

In the pilot it reduced the heat bought at day rates by about a fifth. The update installed itself overnight in all fifty homes.

March 10, 2026 · Research

Why phase-change? A short guide to storing heat

Hot water, hot bricks or a melting salt: the three ways to store heat at home, and why we chose the third.

ReadClose

A hot water tank stores heat well but takes a lot of space for the energy it holds. Bricks heated to several hundred degrees hold more, but need careful insulation and safety design. A phase-change material stores most of its energy as it melts, at a single moderate temperature.

For a store that sits in a closet and feeds radiators at around 140°F, melting at 136°F is the sweet spot. That is why every Heatloop is built around it.

January 27, 2026 · Installs

Pilot homes come through the cold snap

A week of deep cold tested every store in the pilot. Here is what happened, house by house.

ReadClose

For seven days in January the temperature in Bangor did not climb above 10°F. Every pilot home kept its usual comfort settings and none ran short of hot water.

Eleven homes needed some direct heating at day rates on the coldest evenings. We have since adjusted their schedules, and in two cases recommended a larger store.
