How it started

Celandine Vennick learned to throw at night classes in Mesilla while working as a set designer. Ten years of weekends later she had a shed full of bowls, a waiting list of friends, and a lease on the empty shop at 228 N Mesquite.

The first year was mugs, mostly. Then the pitchers, which people kept asking for, and the Ripple bowl, which has been in every kiln since. Today Celandine throws most of what we sell, and Tobi Akintola runs the studio, the workshops and the kiln schedule.

We buy a buff stoneware clay with a little iron in it, which is where the freckles in our chalk and oat glazes come from. The glazes we mix ourselves from raw materials, in small buckets, and test on tiles every time a new bag of anything arrives. It’s slow. It’s also why a cobalt mug from 2019 still matches one from this week.

“If a bowl comes out of the kiln with a glaze run, we don’t toss it. It goes on the seconds shelf, and someone always loves that one most.”

From bag to table

How a piece is made

About three weeks, start to finish. Most of that is waiting.

  1. 01

    Wedge

    Kneading the clay to push out the air and line up the particles. Ten minutes for every bag, by hand.

  2. 02

    Throw

    Centered, opened and pulled up on the wheel. A good day is forty mugs or twelve pitchers.

  3. 03

    Trim and handle

    At leather-hard, the foot is carved and handles are pulled and joined. Then everything dries slowly under plastic.

  4. 04

    Bisque firing

    The first firing, to 1,830°F, turns clay into something hard enough to glaze but still thirsty.

  5. 05

    Glaze

    Dipped by hand, two seconds for a liner, three for the outside. The foot is waxed so the raw clay shows.

  6. 06

    Glaze firing

    To 2,300°F, then a day and a half to cool. We open the kiln together on the first Saturday of the month.
